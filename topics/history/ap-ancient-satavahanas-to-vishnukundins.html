Actual study notes — Ancient Andhra: Satavahanas to Vishnukundins

Historical setting

Ancient Andhra history is reconstructed through inscriptions, coins, archaeology, Buddhist sites and literary traditions. The lower Krishna–Godavari region and interior Deccan were connected by agriculture, craft production, river routes and maritime trade.

Satavahanas

The Satavahanas became a major Deccan power after the Mauryas. Simuka is regarded as an early founder. Gautamiputra Satakarni, known especially from the Nasik inscription of Gautami Balashri, restored power against the Western Kshatrapas. Vasisthiputra Pulumavi was another major ruler.

Prakrit inscriptions, coinage, guild activity and long-distance trade are important. Buddhist establishments flourished through royal, mercantile and lay patronage. Amaravati became one of the greatest Buddhist art centres of the region.

Ikshvakus of Vijayapuri

After Satavahana decline, the Ikshvakus ruled from Vijayapuri in the Nagarjunakonda region. Inscriptions reveal royal patronage of Brahmanical rites alongside extensive Buddhist donations, including important patronage by royal women. Nagarjunakonda became a major Buddhist and architectural centre.

Salankayanas

The Salankayanas ruled parts of the Vengi region. Their inscriptions help trace the political transition between earlier Ikshvaku/Satavahana contexts and later dynasties in coastal Andhra.

Vishnukundins

The Vishnukundins emerged as an important power in the eastern Deccan in the fifth–sixth centuries CE. They expanded across parts of Andhra and Telangana and patronised Brahmanical religious institutions. Their period is important for understanding the transition toward early-medieval regional states.

Culture and historical significance

The period saw interaction among Buddhism, Brahmanical traditions and local cults, development of Prakrit and Sanskrit epigraphy, expanding agrarian settlements and strong links between inland centres and coastal trade.
