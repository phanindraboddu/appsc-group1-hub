History / Vijayanagara Empire

Vijayanagara Empire — Complete APPSC Notes

Prelims + Mains • Andhra-focused • Actual study content

1. Foundation and historical background

The Vijayanagara Empire arose in the fourteenth-century Deccan amid major political changes following the weakening of older southern kingdoms and the expansion of the Delhi Sultanate into the Deccan. Harihara I and Bukka I founded the kingdom in 1336 according to the conventional chronology. Its capital developed at Vijayanagara (Hampi) on the Tungabhadra.

The empire became one of the most powerful states of south India and competed especially with the Bahmani Sultanate and its successor states for strategic zones such as the Raichur Doab and for influence over the eastern coast.

3. Sangama rulers

Harihara I and Bukka I consolidated territory around the Tungabhadra region. Bukka's period saw expansion and the growing importance of Vijayanagara in south Indian politics. Devaraya I and especially Devaraya II strengthened the state, dealt with Bahmani rivalry and supported literary-cultural activity.

4. Krishnadevaraya (1509–1529)

Krishnadevaraya of the Tuluva dynasty represents the political and cultural high point of Vijayanagara. His reign is exceptionally important for APPSC because of his campaigns and close connections with Telugu country.

Military and political achievements

  • Defeated rivals in the Deccan and strengthened imperial authority.
  • His conflict with the Gajapatis was closely connected with control of the eastern Andhra region.
  • Captured the strategically important Raichur Doab from Bijapur in 1520.
  • Maintained relations with Portuguese traders, whose presence on the west coast affected horses, firearms and commerce.

Krishnadevaraya and Andhra

  • His eastern campaigns involved important Andhra forts and territories such as Udayagiri and Kondavidu.
  • He was a major patron of Telugu literature.
  • His Telugu work Amuktamalyada is one of the highest-yield APPSC facts.
  • The famous group of Telugu poets known as the Ashtadiggajas is traditionally associated with his court.

5. Sources

  • Inscriptions and temple records — grants, rulers, institutions and chronology.
  • Abdur Razzaq — Persian visitor who described Vijayanagara in the fifteenth century.
  • Domingo Paes and Fernao Nuniz — Portuguese accounts important for the sixteenth-century capital, court and society.
  • Amuktamalyada — literary source associated with Krishnadevaraya.
  • Architecture and archaeology at Hampi provide major material evidence.

Trap: foreign travellers visited at different times; do not treat all their accounts as descriptions of Krishnadevaraya's reign.

6. Administration

The king stood at the apex of government, assisted by ministers, military commanders and provincial/local authorities. The empire incorporated diverse regional elites rather than operating through one perfectly uniform structure.

Nayankara / Amaram system

Military chiefs or nayakas could receive territorial revenue assignments commonly discussed as amaram. In return they maintained troops and rendered military/service obligations to the king. Nayakas exercised substantial local authority, and some later became increasingly autonomous.

Prelims trap: amaram was not simply modern private property; understand it in the context of revenue assignment, military service and political authority.

7. Economy

  • Agriculture and land revenue formed the economic foundation.
  • Irrigation tanks, canals and reservoirs supported cultivation in the relatively dry interior.
  • The empire participated actively in Indian Ocean commerce.
  • Important trade included textiles, spices, precious goods and especially imported horses required for cavalry.
  • Markets and urban activity at the capital impressed foreign visitors.
  • Temple complexes could function as religious, social and economic institutions.

8. Society and religion

Vijayanagara society was socially differentiated and regionally diverse. Shaiva and Vaishnava traditions received extensive patronage, while Jain and other communities continued to exist. The empire's political identity should not be reduced to a simplistic single-religion narrative; rulers operated in a complex Deccan environment involving diplomacy, warfare and exchange across religious boundaries.

9. Art and architecture

  • Hampi is the principal monumental landscape.
  • Virupaksha Temple — major sacred centre with a long history.
  • Vittala Temple — famous for its monumental complex and iconic stone chariot.
  • Hazara Rama Temple — noted for narrative reliefs.
  • Characteristic features include monumental gateways/gopurams, pillared halls, temple complexes and royal architecture.

The remains reveal both sacred and royal/urban zones and are crucial material sources for understanding the empire.

10. Literature

Royal patronage encouraged Sanskrit and major south Indian languages, especially Telugu and Kannada. Krishnadevaraya himself composed Amuktamalyada in Telugu. Telugu literary tradition associates his court with the Ashtadiggajas; Allasani Peddana is traditionally celebrated as Andhra Kavita Pitamaha. This cultural dimension is especially important for APPSC Mains.

11. Vijayanagara, Bahmanis and Deccan politics

The Vijayanagara–Bahmani rivalry involved control of resources, forts, routes and contested zones including the Raichur Doab. After the Bahmani kingdom fragmented, Vijayanagara dealt with successor sultanates such as Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, Bidar and Berar. Alliances were fluid; political competition cannot be explained solely through religion.

12. Battle of Talikota and aftermath

In 1565, forces of major Deccan sultanates defeated Vijayanagara forces in the Battle of Talikota/Rakshasa-Tangadi. Aliya Rama Raya was a dominant political figure in the period preceding the battle. Hampi was subsequently devastated.

Major trap: 1565 did not instantly end the Vijayanagara state. The Aravidu dynasty continued the political tradition from other centres after the capital's destruction.

13. Andhra Pradesh linkage — very important

  • Vijayanagara politics repeatedly involved Rayalaseema and coastal Andhra.
  • Krishnadevaraya's Gajapati campaigns included major Andhra strongholds such as Udayagiri and Kondavidu.
  • Telugu warriors, administrators, merchants and literary figures participated in the wider imperial system.
  • Telugu literature received major patronage; Amuktamalyada and the Ashtadiggaja tradition are core APPSC areas.
  • Vijayanagara must be connected with Kakatiya successor politics, Reddis, Gajapatis and Qutb Shahis when writing Andhra-centred answers.

15. Prelims high-yield traps

  1. Capital: Vijayanagara/Hampi, on the Tungabhadra.
  2. Dynastic order: Sangama → Saluva → Tuluva → Aravidu.
  3. Krishnadevaraya: Tuluva dynasty, not Sangama.
  4. Amuktamalyada: Telugu work of Krishnadevaraya.
  5. Raichur Doab: strategically contested Krishna–Tungabhadra region.
  6. Nayankara/amaram: military-service/revenue relationship; not simple private ownership.
  7. Paes/Nuniz: Portuguese chroniclers/travellers; Abdur Razzaq: Persian visitor.
  8. Talikota: 1565.
  9. Talikota devastated the capital but did not immediately terminate the dynasty/state.
  10. Andhra connection: Udayagiri, Kondavidu, Telugu literary patronage.

17. Mains practice + answer skeletons

Q1. Assess the achievements of Krishnadevaraya.

Intro → Tuluva accession → military campaigns/Raichur → Gajapati-Andhra campaigns → administration/economy → literature/Amuktamalyada → architecture/patronage → balanced legacy.

Q2. Explain the administrative and socio-economic structure of Vijayanagara.

Monarchy → provincial/local authority → nayankara/amaram → revenue/agriculture → irrigation → trade/horses → markets/temples → strengths and centrifugal tendencies.

Q3. Discuss Vijayanagara's contribution to Telugu culture.

Andhra political link → royal patronage → Krishnadevaraya → Amuktamalyada → Ashtadiggajas/Peddana → temple-cultural networks → lasting significance.

Q4. Did Talikota end the Vijayanagara Empire?

1565 context → alliance of Deccan sultanates → defeat/death of Rama Raya → destruction of Hampi → political weakening → Aravidu continuation → distinguish fall of capital from immediate end of state.
