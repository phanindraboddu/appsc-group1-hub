Full chapter notes

Foundation and chronology

The Mughal Empire was founded by Babur after defeating Ibrahim Lodi at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526. For APPSC, build the core sequence: Babur → Humayun → Akbar → Jahangir → Shah Jahan → Aurangzeb.

Babur and Humayun

  • Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodi at Panipat (1526), Rana Sanga at Khanwa (1527), and strengthened his position in north India.
  • His memoir, the Baburnama, is an important autobiographical source.
  • Humayun lost power to Sher Shah Suri, spent years in exile, and recovered Delhi shortly before his death.
  • Sher Shah's interlude is important for administrative and revenue developments later associated with imperial governance.

Akbar

  • Akbar succeeded in 1556; Bairam Khan served as regent during the early phase.
  • The Second Battle of Panipat (1556) consolidated Mughal recovery against Hemu.
  • Expansion combined conquest, alliances and incorporation of regional elites, including many Rajput houses.
  • Mansabdari: ranked imperial service; mansabdars had status and military/service obligations. Distinguish mansab (rank) from jagir (revenue assignment).
  • Zabt/dahsala: revenue arrangements associated especially with Akbar's administration and Raja Todar Mal; avoid reducing the diverse empire to one uniform revenue method.
  • Akbar's policy of sulh-i kul is associated with universal peace/tolerance in imperial political thought.

Jahangir and Shah Jahan

Jahangir's reign is important for court culture, painting and imperial politics; his memoir is the Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri. Shah Jahan's reign is renowned for monumental architecture, including the Taj Mahal and the imperial complex at Shahjahanabad.

Aurangzeb and the Deccan

  • Aurangzeb ruled for nearly five decades and expanded Mughal territorial control deeply into the Deccan.
  • Bijapur and Golconda were annexed during his reign.
  • Long Deccan wars and conflict with the Marathas imposed major military and fiscal pressures.
  • Explanations for later Mughal decline should be multi-causal: succession conflicts, jagirdari/mansabdari pressures, regional powers, fiscal-military strain and changing political networks.

Administration

The emperor stood at the apex of a complex bureaucracy. High-yield institutions include mansabdari, jagirdari, revenue administration, provincial subahs, and offices concerned with finance and military affairs. Mughal rule relied on negotiation and incorporation of diverse elites as well as coercive power.

Economy and society

Agriculture was the fiscal base. The period also saw substantial craft production, textile manufacture, urban centres and inland/oceanic commerce. Peasants, zamindars, merchants, artisans and imperial officials occupied different positions within the economic order.

Art, architecture and literature

  • Mughal painting developed through Persian and Indian artistic interaction, especially under Akbar and Jahangir.
  • Architecture: Humayun's Tomb, Fatehpur Sikri, Agra Fort, Taj Mahal, Red Fort and Jama Masjid are key examples.
  • Persian was central to imperial administration and high culture, while regional literary traditions continued to flourish.

Andhra/Deccan linkage

For APPSC, connect Mughal expansion to the Deccan sultanates and the Qutb Shahis of Golconda. Aurangzeb's annexation of Golconda in 1687 ended Qutb Shahi sovereignty and directly links Mughal history with the history of the Telugu region.
