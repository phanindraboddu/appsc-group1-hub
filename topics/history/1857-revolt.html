Full chapter notes

Nature and background

The Revolt of 1857 was a major armed uprising against East India Company rule. It began among sepoys but spread in several regions through the participation of dispossessed rulers, taluqdars, peasants, townspeople and others. Its character and geographical intensity varied greatly, so avoid one-dimensional descriptions.

Causes

Political

  • Annexations, the Doctrine of Lapse and the displacement or insecurity of ruling families.
  • The annexation of Awadh in 1856 generated particular resentment among sections of the elite, taluqdars and soldiers connected with the region.

Economic and agrarian

  • Revenue pressures, changes in landed relationships and grievances among peasants and dispossessed intermediaries varied by region.
  • Artisans and older elites also faced changes produced by colonial political economy.

Military

  • Indian sepoys had grievances concerning pay, allowances, service conditions and status.
  • The immediate cartridge controversy became a powerful trigger because it intersected with religious fears and wider mistrust.

Socio-religious anxieties

Some groups feared colonial interference with religion and social customs. These anxieties must be understood alongside, not instead of, political, military and economic causes.

Outbreak and centres

The uprising erupted at Meerut on 10 May 1857. Rebels moved to Delhi and proclaimed the ageing Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah II as a symbolic leader.

Why it did not succeed

  • Geographically uneven spread; large areas and many rulers/groups did not join.
  • No single unified command or common programme for post-Company governance.
  • British access to military resources, communications and reinforcements.
  • Different participants fought for overlapping but not identical objectives.

Nature of the Revolt: historiographical caution

Interpretations have ranged from a “sepoy mutiny” to a broader popular rebellion and a “first war of independence.” For Mains, do not solve the debate with a slogan. State that it began as a military revolt, acquired broader civil/popular dimensions in important regions, remained geographically uneven, and united diverse grievances rather than a single modern national programme.

Consequences

  • The Government of India Act 1858 ended East India Company rule and transferred authority to the British Crown.
  • The office of Secretary of State for India became central to imperial governance.
  • Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1858 announced important principles regarding princely states, religion and governance.
  • The army was reorganised and British policy toward princes, landlords and communities was recalibrated.
  • The Mughal dynasty formally ended; Bahadur Shah II was exiled.

Andhra perspective

The main military centres of 1857 were concentrated in north and central India; the Telugu region did not experience an uprising on the same scale. For APPSC, distinguish the Revolt of 1857 from earlier and later resistance in Andhra rather than artificially assigning the major north Indian pattern to the region.
