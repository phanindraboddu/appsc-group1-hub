Kanauj and Early Medieval India

APPSC Group-I • Prelims + Mains integrated • Full chapter

1. Historical setting

The decline of Gupta political authority produced several regional powers in north India. Important ruling houses included the Maukharis of Kanauj, Later Guptas of Magadha, Gaudas of Bengal, Maitrakas of Valabhi and Pushyabhutis of Thanesar. This was not simply an age of “decline”: regional state formation, agrarian expansion, temple institutions and regional cultures became increasingly important.

2. Why Kanauj became important

  • Kanauj (Kanyakubja) lay strategically in the fertile Ganga plain and became a major political centre after the Gupta age.
  • Control of Kanauj carried both strategic value and imperial prestige in north India.
  • The political centre of gravity increasingly shifted away from Pataliputra toward Kanauj.
  • Its importance under Harsha later made it the focus of the famous three-way contest among the Palas, Gurjara-Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas.

3. Pushyabhutis and Harshavardhana

The Pushyabhutis rose around Thanesar. Harshavardhana came to power in 606 CE after a dynastic crisis involving his brother Rajyavardhana and sister Rajyashri. Harsha subsequently made Kanauj the centre of his power and became the most prominent ruler of seventh-century north India.

Important sources

  • Banabhatta — Harshacharita: court biography; valuable but eulogistic.
  • Xuanzang: Chinese Buddhist pilgrim whose travel account supplies information on political, social and religious conditions.
  • Banskhera and Madhuban copper plates and other inscriptions/coins provide epigraphic evidence.
  • Always distinguish court literature, foreign travel accounts and inscriptions as different types of historical evidence.

4. Harsha's political career

  • Harsha consolidated power in much of north India and formed an alliance with Bhaskaravarman of Kamarupa.
  • His attempt to extend power south of the Narmada was checked by the Chalukya ruler Pulakeshin II.
  • Therefore, avoid the trap that Harsha conquered the whole Indian subcontinent.
  • He maintained diplomatic contacts beyond his kingdom, including contacts with China.
  • Harsha died in 647 CE; no durable empire of comparable scale survived him.

5. Administration and polity

  • The king remained the central political figure, assisted by ministers and officials.
  • Land grants and powerful subordinate rulers or samantas became important features of political organisation.
  • Regional and local power-holders could exercise substantial authority; early-medieval polity should not be imagined as uniformly centralised.
  • Land grants to religious beneficiaries and officials contributed to changing fiscal and agrarian relations.

6. Society, economy and agrarian expansion

  • Expansion of cultivation into new areas was linked with settlements, land grants and incorporation of local communities.
  • Brahmanical beneficiaries and religious establishments often became important nodes in rural society.
  • Temples increasingly functioned not merely as religious centres but also as landholders, patrons and centres of social/economic activity in many regions.
  • Historians debate the extent of urban decline, trade contraction and “Indian feudalism”; in Mains answers present these as debates with regional variation rather than settled uniform facts.

7. Religion, learning and culture under Harsha

  • Harsha is associated with patronage across religious traditions; Xuanzang records his Buddhist patronage, while other evidence shows a broader religious environment.
  • Assemblies at Kanauj and Prayaga are associated with his reign.
  • Nalanda remained a major centre of Buddhist learning visited by Xuanzang.
  • Harsha is traditionally credited with Sanskrit plays such as Ratnavali, Priyadarsika and Nagananda.

8. After Harsha: regionalisation

After Harsha's death, north India did not remain under a single dominant dynasty. Numerous regional states competed for territory and prestige. This political regionalisation accompanied the growth of distinctive regional cultures, ruling lineages, temple traditions and agrarian networks.

9. Tripartite Struggle for Kanauj

From the eighth century, three major powers repeatedly competed for supremacy in north India:

PowerCore regionHigh-yield rulers
Gurjara-PratiharasWestern/central north IndiaVatsaraja, Nagabhata II, Mihira Bhoja
PalasBengal and Bihar/eastern IndiaGopala, Dharmapala, Devapala
RashtrakutasDeccanDhruva, Govinda III

Why Kanauj? Its Ganga-plain location, resources, communications and accumulated imperial prestige made control of it a symbol of paramount power.

Basic sequence

  • Gurjara-Pratihara Vatsaraja and Pala Dharmapala were early competitors.
  • Rashtrakuta Dhruva intervened from the Deccan and defeated northern rivals but did not establish permanent northern control.
  • Dharmapala exercised influence around Kanauj and supported Chakrayudha.
  • Pratihara Nagabhata II subsequently asserted control over Kanauj; Rashtrakuta Govinda III also campaigned north.
  • The contest continued in changing forms; no single power maintained uncontested supremacy throughout the struggle.

10. The three powers — contributions

Palas

Strong in eastern India and noted for patronage of Mahayana Buddhism and centres such as Nalanda and Vikramashila. Pala art developed a distinctive sculptural/manuscript tradition.

Gurjara-Pratiharas

A major north Indian power, especially under rulers such as Mihira Bhoja. Their political position helped check rival expansion and sustain a large north Indian kingdom.

Rashtrakutas

A major Deccan imperial power whose northern interventions made the Kanauj contest genuinely inter-regional. Their wider cultural legacy includes major patronage of art and literature.

11. Andhra/Deccan linkage

  • The Rashtrakutas demonstrate that north Indian politics cannot be studied separately from the Deccan.
  • Rashtrakuta relations with the Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi connect this wider imperial competition to Andhra history.
  • For APPSC Mains, use this to show the inter-regional character of early-medieval political networks rather than treating Andhra history as isolated.

12. Prelims high-yield facts & traps

  1. Harsha belonged to the Pushyabhuti/Vardhana dynasty.
  2. Earlier Pushyabhuti centre: Thanesar; Harsha became associated with Kanauj.
  3. Harsha's accession: 606 CE.
  4. Harsha's southward expansion was checked by Pulakeshin II.
  5. Harshacharita — Banabhatta.
  6. Chinese pilgrim at Harsha's time — Xuanzang.
  7. Tripartite struggle: Palas + Gurjara-Pratiharas + Rashtrakutas.
  8. Palas — eastern India; Pratiharas — western/northern India; Rashtrakutas — Deccan.
  9. Gopala founded the Pala line; Dharmapala became a major participant in the Kanauj struggle.
  10. Do not equate political regionalisation automatically with cultural/economic decline.

13. Practice MCQs

  1. The author of Harshacharita was: (A) Kalhana (B) Banabhatta (C) Bilhana (D) Dandin
    Answer: B
  2. Harsha's advance towards the Deccan was checked by: (A) Narasimhavarman I (B) Pulakeshin II (C) Dantidurga (D) Amoghavarsha
    Answer: B
  3. The tripartite struggle centred on control of: (A) Ujjain (B) Pataliputra (C) Kanauj (D) Thanesar
    Answer: C
  4. Which was NOT a participant in the tripartite struggle? (A) Palas (B) Pratiharas (C) Rashtrakutas (D) Cholas
    Answer: D
  5. Dharmapala belonged to the: (A) Pala (B) Pratihara (C) Rashtrakuta (D) Chalukya dynasty
    Answer: A
  6. Xuanzang is especially important as a source for the reign of: (A) Ashoka (B) Harsha (C) Kanishka (D) Samudragupta
    Answer: B
  7. Match correctly: Kanauj—Kanyakubja; Thanesar—Pushyabhutis; Bengal—Palas; Deccan—Rashtrakutas.
    Answer: All correctly matched.
  8. The rise of land grants in early medieval India is most directly studied in relation to: (A) agrarian and political change (B) only maritime trade (C) only coinage (D) only foreign invasion
    Answer: A
  9. Which statement is safest? (A) Harsha ruled all India (B) Early medieval India had no trade (C) Regional powers became important after Gupta decline (D) Kanauj lost all political importance after Harsha
    Answer: C
  10. The Deccan power intervening repeatedly in the Kanauj struggle was the: (A) Palas (B) Rashtrakutas (C) Senas (D) Maukharis
    Answer: B

14. Mains questions & answer skeletons

Q1. Explain the rise of Kanauj as the political centre of early medieval north India.

Post-Gupta fragmentation → geographic/strategic position → Harsha's association → imperial prestige → Ganga resources/routes → post-Harsha contest → tripartite struggle → conclusion on changing political geography.

Q2. Discuss Harsha's political and cultural significance.

Sources → accession → consolidation/alliance → Pulakeshin II limit → administration/samantas → religion/learning/literature → Xuanzang → limits of empire → balanced conclusion.

Q3. Analyse the Tripartite Struggle.

Kanauj significance → three powers/core regions → principal rulers/interventions → changing control → Deccan-north linkage → political consequences → no simplistic permanent victor narrative.

Q4. Was early medieval India merely an age of political fragmentation?

Regional kingdoms → decentralised authority → land grants → agrarian expansion → temple institutions → regional cultures → trade/urbanisation debate → conclude “regional transformation”, not merely decline.
