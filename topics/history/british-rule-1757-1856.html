Full chapter notes

From Company trade to territorial rule

The period from Plassey (1757) to the eve of the Revolt of 1857 saw the East India Company transform from a trading corporation into a territorial and revenue-collecting power. The process was gradual and regionally uneven.

Bengal: the decisive fiscal base

  • Plassey (1757): strengthened Company political influence in Bengal.
  • Buxar (1764): Company victory over the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula and Mughal emperor Shah Alam II.
  • Diwani (1765): the Company obtained revenue-collection rights in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, giving it a powerful fiscal base.

Expansion of Company power

The Company expanded through warfare, alliances, annexation and control over successor states. Important conflicts included the Anglo-Mysore, Anglo-Maratha and Anglo-Sikh wars.

  • Subsidiary Alliance: especially associated with Lord Wellesley; Indian rulers accepted Company troops and restrictions that reduced their external autonomy.
  • Doctrine of Lapse: associated with Lord Dalhousie and used to annex certain states where the Company refused to recognise adopted heirs for succession to rulership.
  • Awadh: annexed in 1856 on the stated ground of misgovernment, not under the Doctrine of Lapse.

Economic transformation

  • Colonial revenue demands altered agrarian relations and could intensify pressure on cultivators.
  • India became increasingly integrated into an empire-centred trading system supplying raw materials and consuming manufactured goods.
  • Traditional crafts experienced uneven changes; avoid the oversimplification that every craft everywhere disappeared at the same rate.
  • Infrastructure such as railways and telegraph later served multiple functions, including administration, military movement and commerce.

Administrative and legal changes

Parliament progressively regulated Company rule through measures including the Regulating Act (1773), Pitt's India Act (1784), and successive Charter Acts. Administrative centralisation, courts, codification and a professional bureaucracy developed over time.

Education and social legislation

  • The nineteenth century saw expanding debate over Orientalist and Anglicist approaches to education.
  • Macaulay's Minute (1835) and Wood's Despatch (1854) are high-yield milestones.
  • The abolition of sati (1829) under Governor-General William Bentinck and the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (1856) belong to a wider field of reform involving Indian reformers, colonial legislation and social contestation.

Andhra/Madras Presidency connection

Much of the Telugu region came under Company influence through the Madras Presidency and related territorial arrangements. Ryotwari settlement is particularly important for south India. The Northern Circars and Ceded Districts form essential background to the expansion of British authority in Andhra; study their acquisition alongside the decline/reorganisation of older regional powers.
