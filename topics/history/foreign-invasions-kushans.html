Exam-ready core

  • After the Mauryas, north-western India saw Indo-Greek, Shaka, Parthian and Kushan political formations; learn their sequence without treating each as a simple one-time invasion.
  • Indo-Greeks: Menander/Milinda is associated with the Buddhist dialogue Milindapanho; bilingual coinage is important evidence.
  • Shakas: Rudradaman I and the Junagadh inscription are high-yield; the inscription is notable for its elaborate Sanskrit prose.
  • Kushans: Kanishka is the best-known ruler; Kushan power connected north India with Central Asian trade networks.
  • Gandhara and Mathura developed as major artistic centres; compare material, iconography and artistic conventions rather than memorising a simplistic “foreign vs Indian” contrast.
  • Kushan coinage reflects extensive commerce and a diverse religious-cultural environment.

Actual study notes — Foreign Invasions & Kushans

North-western contacts

The north-west connected the subcontinent with Iranian and Central Asian worlds. Achaemenid expansion reached parts of the north-west; Alexander invaded in 326 BCE and fought Porus on the Hydaspes. His campaign did not establish lasting Greek rule over India.

Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Parthians

After the Mauryas, several groups established kingdoms in the north-west and western India. Indo-Greek ruler Menander (Milinda) is associated with the Buddhist dialogue text Milindapanho. Shaka political power became important in western India; Rudradaman I's Junagadh inscription is a major Sanskrit epigraphic source.

Kushans

The Kushans emerged from Central Asian Yuezhi groups and built a large trans-regional empire. Kanishka is the best-known ruler, though the precise chronology of Kushan rulers has scholarly complexities.

Economy and Silk Routes

Kushan territory linked India, Central Asia and long-distance routes. Coinage, trade and urban centres illustrate a highly connected economy. Roman trade also formed part of the wider commercial environment.

Religion and culture

  • Kushan patronage was religiously diverse.
  • Kanishka is strongly associated in Buddhist tradition with Mahayana patronage.
  • Gandhara and Mathura became major artistic centres; distinguish their materials and stylistic environments rather than calling one simply “foreign” and the other “Indian”.
