Actual study notes — Mughals, Marathas & Europeans

Mughal sequence

Babur defeated Ibrahim Lodi at the First Battle of Panipat (1526). Humayun lost power to Sher Shah before recovering it. Akbar consolidated and expanded the empire; Jahangir and Shah Jahan presided over continued imperial power; Aurangzeb's long reign brought major Deccan expansion and prolonged warfare.

Mughal administration

Mansab denoted rank/service position while jagir was a revenue assignment; they are related but not identical. Akbar's revenue administration is associated with Todar Mal and the zabt/dahsala system in areas where measurement and assessment were feasible.

Deccan and Andhra

Mughal pressure on the Deccan intensified under Aurangzeb. Bijapur was annexed in 1686 and Golconda in 1687, directly connecting Mughal imperial expansion with the political history of the Telugu region.

Marathas

Shivaji created a powerful western Deccan kingdom through forts, mobile warfare, revenue organisation and political state-building. His coronation took place in 1674. The later Maratha confederacy became a major eighteenth-century power.

Europeans

Portuguese maritime entry followed Vasco da Gama's arrival at Calicut in 1498. Dutch, English and French companies subsequently competed in Indian Ocean commerce. The English East India Company was chartered in 1600. Commercial rivalry gradually acquired a territorial-political dimension, especially in the eighteenth century.

Andhra coast

Machilipatnam and the Coromandel textile world linked Telugu regions with European companies and Asian trade networks. European commerce operated within existing Indian merchant and political systems before colonial dominance developed.
