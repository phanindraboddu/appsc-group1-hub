Jainism & Buddhism — Complete APPSC Chapter

APPSC Group-I • Prelims + Mains • Ancient India + Andhra focus

1. Why did Jainism & Buddhism arise?

  • Both belonged to the wider śramaṇa tradition and became prominent around the 6th century BCE.
  • The background included the growth of towns and trade, new social groups, territorial states and intellectual questioning of ritual-centred religious practices.
  • The middle Gangetic region was a major setting for these movements.
  • Exam approach: do not explain their rise through one cause alone. Combine socio-economic change, political change and religious-philosophical questioning.

2. Jainism — Tirthankaras & Mahavira

  • Jain tradition recognises 24 Tirthankaras. Rishabhanatha is the first; Parshvanatha the 23rd; Vardhamana Mahavira the 24th.
  • Mahavira was a contemporary of the Buddha and is associated with the Vajji region of north India.
  • He renounced worldly life, practised severe asceticism and attained kevala jnana according to Jain tradition.
  • Jainism did not begin simply as a creation of Mahavira; he was the 24th Tirthankara within an older tradition.

3. Core Jain Philosophy

  • Triratna / Three Jewels: Right Faith (samyak darshana), Right Knowledge (samyak jnana), Right Conduct (samyak charitra).
  • Five vows: ahimsa, satya, asteya, aparigraha and brahmacharya.
  • Ahimsa occupies an especially rigorous position in Jain ethics.
  • Anekantavada: reality has multiple aspects; a single standpoint may not exhaust truth.
  • Syadvada: conditional/perspectival predication, associated with Jain epistemology.
  • Jain thought accepts individual souls (jiva), karma and rebirth; liberation requires release from karmic bondage through disciplined ethical and ascetic practice.

4. Jain Sects & Literature

  • The two major traditions are Digambara and Svetambara.
  • Differences concern monastic practice, clothing, canonical traditions and some doctrinal/historical interpretations.
  • Early Jain teaching was transmitted orally; Jain canonical literature is strongly associated with Prakrit/Ardhamagadhi traditions.
  • The council tradition, including the later Vallabhi compilation associated with Svetambara canon, should be studied with awareness that sectarian traditions preserve different accounts.

5. Gautama Buddha — Life

  • Siddhartha Gautama belonged to the Shakya clan.
  • Key sacred geography: Lumbini—birth; Bodh Gaya—enlightenment; Sarnath—first sermon; Kushinagara—mahaparinirvana.
  • After enlightenment he became the Buddha, “the Awakened One,” and taught across the Gangetic region.
  • His community developed into the Sangha of monks and nuns, alongside lay followers.

6. Core Buddhist Doctrine

  • Four Noble Truths: suffering/unsatisfactoriness (dukkha); its origin; its cessation; and the path leading to cessation.
  • Noble Eightfold Path: right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration.
  • Middle Path: rejection of both sensual indulgence and extreme self-mortification.
  • Dependent origination (pratityasamutpada): phenomena arise dependently through conditions.
  • Anitya/anicca: impermanence; anatman/anatta: absence of an eternal unchanging self; nirvana: liberation from the cycle driven by craving and ignorance.

7. Buddhist Sangha, Texts & Councils

  • Tripitaka/Tipitaka: Vinaya Pitaka—monastic discipline; Sutta/Sutra Pitaka—discourses; Abhidhamma/Abhidharma—systematic doctrinal analysis.
  • Traditional accounts associate the First Council with Rajagriha after the Buddha's death and the Second with Vaishali.
  • A Third Council is traditionally associated in Theravada sources with Pataliputra and Ashoka.
  • A later council associated with Kanishka appears in northern Buddhist traditions. Council accounts vary among traditions, so avoid presenting every traditional detail as uncontested history.

8. Major Buddhist Traditions

  • Theravada: preserves the Pali canon and remains prominent in Sri Lanka and much of Southeast Asia.
  • Mahayana: gives major importance to the Bodhisattva ideal and developed a large body of additional sutra literature.
  • Vajrayana: later tantric/esoteric Buddhist traditions using distinctive ritual and meditative practices.
  • Exam caution: “Hinayana” is found in older exam books but is not a precise synonym for Theravada; prefer the historically appropriate tradition name.

9. Why did Buddhism spread widely?

  • Monastic institutions and organised Sangha networks.
  • Patronage by rulers, merchants and other donors.
  • Links with towns, trade routes and commercial networks.
  • Missionary and intellectual activity, including expansion beyond the subcontinent.
  • Ashoka was a major patron, but Buddhism existed and spread before him; do not make Ashoka its sole cause.

10. Ashoka & Buddhism

  • Ashoka's inscriptions are crucial contemporary sources.
  • His dhamma emphasised ethical conduct, respect, restraint and social harmony. It should not simply be equated with the complete doctrinal system of Buddhism.
  • Ashoka's personal association with Buddhism and his wider public policy of dhamma should therefore be distinguished in exam answers.

11. Buddhist Art & Architecture

  • Stupa: relic/memorial monument; learn anda, harmika, yasti/chhatra, vedika and torana terminology.
  • Chaitya: worship/prayer hall, often associated with a stupa.
  • Vihara: monastic residence.
  • Sanchi, Bharhut, Amaravati and Nagarjunakonda are high-yield centres.
  • Early Buddhist art often represented the Buddha through symbols; later anthropomorphic Buddha images became widespread.
  • Gandhara: north-western artistic centre with distinctive Greco-Roman-influenced conventions; Mathura: major indigenous sculptural centre using characteristic red sandstone traditions.

12. Buddhism in Andhra — VERY IMPORTANT

  • Amaravati: major Buddhist stupa and sculptural centre on the Krishna; famous for limestone relief sculpture and long phases of patronage.
  • Nagarjunakonda/Vijayapuri: major Buddhist centre, especially important under the Ikshvakus; inscriptions and monuments attest monasteries and diverse Buddhist establishments.
  • Bhattiprolu: early Buddhist stupa/inscriptional importance.
  • Ghantasala: significant lower-Krishna Buddhist centre connected with coastal exchange.
  • Thotlakonda & Bavikonda: important monastic complexes near the Visakhapatnam coast, illustrating Buddhism's relationship with maritime routes.
  • Salihundam: important Buddhist site in north coastal Andhra.
  • For Mains, connect Andhra Buddhism with Krishna-Godavari networks, merchants, ports, monasteries, Satavahana/Ikshvaku-age patronage and Buddhist art.

13. Jainism in Andhra

  • Jainism also formed part of the religious landscape of Andhra and the wider Deccan, supported at different times by rulers, elites, monks and mercantile communities.
  • For APPSC, focus on archaeological/inscriptional evidence and securely identified centres rather than memorising doubtful legends.
  • In comparative answers, note that Buddhism left an especially visible monumental and sculptural record at major Andhra sites such as Amaravati and Nagarjunakonda.

15. Similarities

  • Both emerged within the wider śramaṇa environment.
  • Both questioned the sufficiency of ritual sacrifice and stressed ethical conduct and disciplined practice.
  • Both accepted karma and rebirth, while explaining the person/self differently.
  • Both developed monastic communities and also depended on lay support.
  • Both benefited from commercial networks and patronage and made major contributions to Indian art, architecture and intellectual history.

16. Decline/Transformation in India

  • Avoid single-cause answers. Institutional change, changing patterns of patronage, assimilation/interaction with other traditions, internal developments and political-economic changes varied across regions.
  • Buddhism declined as a major organised religion in much of India but survived and transformed elsewhere in Asia; Jain communities continued in several Indian regions.

17. Prelims High-Yield Traps

  1. Mahavira was the 24th, not first, Tirthankara.
  2. Parshvanatha was the 23rd Tirthankara.
  3. Buddha: Lumbini → Bodh Gaya → Sarnath → Kushinagara.
  4. Vinaya = monastic discipline; Sutta/Sutra = discourses; Abhidhamma = systematic doctrine.
  5. Jainism accepts jiva; Buddhism teaches anatman.
  6. Chaitya ≠ vihara: worship hall vs monastic residence.
  7. Do not equate Ashoka's dhamma mechanically with Buddhist doctrine.
  8. Do not use “Hinayana = Theravada” as an exact equivalence.
  9. Amaravati and Nagarjunakonda are indispensable for APPSC.
  10. Nagarjunakonda is strongly associated with Ikshvaku-period Buddhist patronage.

    Ans: C

    Ans: B

    Ans: B

19. Mains Questions + Answer Skeletons

Q1. Explain the circumstances responsible for the rise of Jainism and Buddhism.
Intro: 6th-century BCE śramaṇa setting → urbanisation/trade/state formation → religious-intellectual questioning → new social constituencies → doctrines/organisation → balanced conclusion.

Q2. Compare the fundamental teachings of Jainism and Buddhism.
Intro → common śramaṇa setting → karma/rebirth/ethics → Jain jiva, Triratna, vows, ahimsa → Buddhist Four Truths, Eightfold Path, anatman, Middle Path → similarities/differences → conclusion.

Q3. Discuss the growth of Buddhism in ancient Andhra and its contribution to art and architecture.
Map-based intro → trade/river/coastal routes → patronage → Amaravati → Nagarjunakonda → Bhattiprolu/Ghantasala/Visakhapatnam sites → stupa/sculpture/monasteries → cultural significance.

Q4. Assess the role of patronage and trade in the spread of Buddhism.
Sangha → merchants/donors → routes/towns/ports → rulers including Ashoka → monasteries as nodes → Andhra examples → avoid monocausal conclusion.

Q5. Explain the cultural contribution of Jainism and Buddhism to India.
Ethics/philosophy → literature/languages → institutions/education → architecture/sculpture → trans-Asian connections → social-cultural legacy.
