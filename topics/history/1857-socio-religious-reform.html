Actual study notes — Revolt of 1857 & Socio-Religious Reform

Causes of 1857

The revolt resulted from interacting political, economic, military and socio-religious grievances. Annexations and dispossession affected rulers and elites; agrarian and economic pressures affected sections of society; sepoy grievances accumulated. The immediate trigger was the cartridge controversy.

Course and centres

The uprising began at Meerut on 10 May 1857 and rebels moved to Delhi, where Bahadur Shah II became a symbolic emperor. Major centres included Kanpur under Nana Sahib, Jhansi under Rani Lakshmibai, Awadh associated with Begum Hazrat Mahal, and Bihar under Kunwar Singh.

Nature and limits

Participation varied sharply by region and social group. Historians have described 1857 variously as a sepoy mutiny, civil rebellion and first war of independence. A strong Mains answer explains the evidence behind these interpretations rather than adopting a slogan without qualification.

Consequences

The Government of India Act 1858 ended East India Company rule and transferred authority to the Crown. Army organisation and policies toward princes changed, and Queen Victoria's Proclamation announced important principles of the new regime.

Reform movements

Rammohan Roy/Brahmo Samaj, Dayanand/Arya Samaj, Phules/Satyashodhak Samaj, Sir Syed/Aligarh movement, Vivekananda/Ramakrishna Mission and regional reformers addressed religion, caste, education, women and social practice in different ways.

Andhra

Andhra did not experience the same scale of 1857 rebellion as the major north Indian centres. Later social reform in Andhra, particularly associated with Kandukuri Veeresalingam, became crucial to modern Telugu public life.
