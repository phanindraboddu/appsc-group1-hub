Integrated revision core

  • Place Jainism and Buddhism in the context of sixth-century BCE social-economic change, urbanisation and questioning of ritual hierarchy.
  • Jainism: Mahavira, triratna, five vows, anekantavada and monastic/lay traditions.
  • Buddhism: Four Noble Truths, Eightfold Path, dependent origination, sangha and spread through patronage/trade networks.
  • Mauryas: Chandragupta, Bindusara and Ashoka; use inscriptions, archaeology, Greek accounts and literary texts carefully as different source types.
  • Ashoka: inscriptions are the principal contemporary evidence for dhamma; distinguish dhamma from a simple synonym for Buddhism.
  • Andhra link: Buddhist expansion and later flourishing at Amaravati/Nagarjunakonda form an important regional continuation.

Actual study notes — Jainism, Buddhism, Mauryas & Ashoka: Integrated Revision

Why new religious movements grew

In the sixth century BCE, urbanisation, expanding trade, new states and intellectual questioning formed the setting for śramaṇa traditions. Jainism and Buddhism challenged the exclusive centrality of Vedic sacrifice and offered disciplined ethical paths to liberation.

Jainism

Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara, taught a path centred on right faith, right knowledge and right conduct. Ahimsa became its most famous ethical principle. Anekantavada stresses the many-sided character of reality; Digambara and Svetambara traditions developed distinct practices and textual histories.

Buddhism

The Buddha taught the Four Noble Truths, Eightfold Path, Middle Way, impermanence and dependent origination. The Sangha helped institutionalise and transmit the teaching. Buddhist traditions later included Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana forms.

Magadha to Mauryas

Magadha's agrarian resources, river communications, strategic location and ambitious rulers supported expansion. Chandragupta Maurya founded the Mauryan Empire; Bindusara succeeded him; Ashoka became its best-documented ruler through inscriptions.

Ashoka

Major Rock Edict XIII describes the Kalinga war and Ashoka's remorse. His dhamma promoted ethical conduct, restraint, respect and welfare. Dhamma should not be mechanically equated with the entirety of Buddhist doctrine.

Art and Andhra

Stupas, chaityas and viharas became central Buddhist architectural forms. Andhra later became a major Buddhist region through sites such as Amaravati, Bhattiprolu and Nagarjunakonda, linked to trade, patronage and monastic networks.

Prelims traps

Mahavira–24th Tirthankara • Buddha–Sarnath first sermon • Chandragupta–Mauryan founder • Megasthenes–Indica • Edict XIII–Kalinga • Ashokan dhamma ≠ simply Buddhist theology.
