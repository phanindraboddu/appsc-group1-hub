1. Importance of Indian Agriculture

Agriculture continues to play a fundamental role in India's economy even though its relative contribution to national income has declined with structural transformation.

Its importance extends beyond crop production because agriculture and allied activities support employment, rural incomes, food security, industrial raw materials and domestic demand.

Large workforce dependence + relatively smaller share of national income → Lower income/productivity per agricultural worker
✍️ MAINS INSIGHT

A declining share of agriculture in GDP does not mean agriculture has become unimportant. The key structural issue is the continued dependence of a large workforce on a sector producing a comparatively smaller share of national output.

4. Cropping Pattern

Cropping pattern refers to the distribution or proportion of cultivated area devoted to different crops during a particular period.

A change from paddy cultivation towards horticulture, pulses or other crops therefore represents a change in cropping pattern.

Major determinants

Nature + Water + Soil + Market + Prices + Technology

Other influences include farm size, availability of agricultural credit, government procurement, dietary demand, infrastructure and government policy.

6. Structural Problems of Indian Agriculture

LAND → WATER → INPUTS → CREDIT → MARKET → PRICE → CLIMATE

Land: Small and fragmented holdings can restrict economies of scale.

Water: Uneven irrigation availability and rainfall dependence.

Inputs: Cost, quality and access issues involving seeds, fertilisers, pesticides and machinery.

Markets: Storage, cold-chain, transport and marketing constraints.

Prices: Price volatility and uneven access to procurement mechanisms.

8. Green Revolution

The Green Revolution was introduced in the context of India's food shortages and aimed at substantially increasing agricultural production and productivity.

HYV Seeds + Irrigation + Fertilisers + Pesticides + Credit + Mechanisation + MSP/Procurement

Major achievements

Higher foodgrain production, improved food self-sufficiency, higher productivity in major beneficiary regions and reduced dependence on food imports.

Major concerns

Regional inequality, concentration on selected crops, groundwater depletion, intensive chemical-input use, soil-related problems and unequal access to the benefits of the new technology.

⚠️ APPSC TRAP

The Green Revolution was not simply the introduction of HYV seeds. It involved a package of technological, financial, irrigation, price-support and institutional measures.

Indian agriculture cannot depend entirely on rainfall because the monsoon is seasonal, unevenly distributed and variable. Irrigation therefore plays an important role in stabilising agricultural production, supporting multiple cropping and reducing rainfall-related risks.

13. Net vs Gross Irrigated Area

Suppose the same one hectare of land receives irrigation for a Kharif crop and again for a Rabi crop.

Net Irrigated Area = 1 hectare

Gross Irrigated Area = 2 hectares

Net irrigated area counts the physical area once, whereas gross irrigated area counts every irrigated crop grown on that land during the year.

🔥 REMEMBER

Gross Irrigated Area can be greater than Net Irrigated Area.

14. Cropping Intensity

Cropping Intensity = (Gross Cropped Area ÷ Net Sown Area) × 100

Example:

Net Sown Area = 100 hectares
Gross Cropped Area = 160 hectares

Cropping Intensity = 160%

Higher cropping intensity broadly indicates that agricultural land is being cultivated more than once during an agricultural year.

⚠️ DON'T CONFUSE

Cropping Intensity ≠ Agricultural Productivity ≠ Irrigation Intensity

15. Irrigation Potential

Irrigation Potential Created: Area capable of receiving irrigation because the required irrigation infrastructure has been created.

Irrigation Potential Utilised: Area actually benefiting from or using the irrigation facilities.

Potential Created ≠ Potential Utilised

The gap may arise due to incomplete distribution systems, maintenance deficiencies, field-channel problems and other command-area constraints.

🌱 22. Agricultural Inputs

Agricultural inputs are the resources used by farmers for crop production and improvement of agricultural productivity. They include seeds, fertilisers, manure, irrigation, machinery, pesticides, electricity, credit and modern technology. Agricultural productivity does not depend merely on increasing the quantity of these inputs, but on using them in the correct combination. The Green Revolution demonstrated that improved seeds produce their full potential only when supported by irrigation, nutrients, plant protection, credit and appropriate institutional support.

Core idea:

Quality Seed → Healthy Soil → Balanced Nutrients → Water → Machinery → Technology → Higher Productivity

🌾 23. Seeds: HYV and Hybrid Seeds

Seed is one of the most important foundations of crop production. Good-quality seed affects germination, plant population, crop duration, resistance characteristics and final yield. Modern agriculture therefore requires an efficient system for development, multiplication, certification, storage and timely supply of quality seeds. For examination purposes, traditional varieties, HYVs, hybrids and genetically modified seeds should not be treated as identical concepts.

High-Yielding Varieties (HYVs) are developed or selected for their ability to produce relatively high yields when suitable inputs and environmental conditions are available. Hybrid seeds, on the other hand, are produced by crossing genetically different parent lines to combine desirable characteristics. Hybrid vigour is known as heterosis.

🎯 Prelims Trap

HYV ≠ Hybrid ≠ GM Seed

Every HYV is not necessarily a hybrid.

🧪 24. Fertilisers and NPK

Plants require several nutrients for normal growth. Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are among the primary macronutrients commonly supplied through fertilisers. Nitrogen plays an important role in vegetative growth and chlorophyll-related processes. Phosphorus is important for energy transfer and root development, while potassium contributes to water regulation, enzyme activity and overall plant vigour. Balanced nutrient application is therefore more important than excessive use of any single fertiliser.

Easy Memory

N → Growth / Leaves 🌿
P → Roots / Energy 🌱
K → Strength / Regulation 💪

Urea is an important nitrogenous fertiliser and contains about 46 per cent nitrogen by weight. Excessive dependence on nitrogen without adequate phosphorus, potassium and other nutrients may cause nutrient imbalance and reduce nutrient-use efficiency. Hence agricultural policy increasingly emphasises soil testing and balanced fertilisation.

🎯 Important Fact

Urea → Nitrogenous fertiliser → approximately 46% Nitrogen

🌿 Integrated Nutrient Management

Integrated Nutrient Management aims to maintain soil fertility and crop productivity through the appropriate combination of chemical fertilisers, organic manures, crop residues, biofertilisers and soil testing. Organic manure contributes organic matter and improves several physical and biological characteristics of soil, while manufactured fertilisers supply nutrients in concentrated forms. Sustainable nutrient management therefore requires complementary rather than indiscriminate use of different nutrient sources.

Input Main Characteristic
Chemical Fertiliser Concentrated manufactured nutrient source
Organic Manure Organic material supplying nutrients and organic matter
Biofertiliser Contains beneficial microorganisms

🧾 25. Soil Health and Farm Mechanisation

Soil testing helps determine the nutrient status of agricultural land and enables crop-specific nutrient recommendations. The Soil Health Card approach seeks to move agriculture away from uniform fertiliser application towards soil-based nutrient management. Proper soil testing can improve nutrient-use efficiency, reduce unnecessary expenditure and contribute to long-term soil health.

Soil Test → Nutrient Status → Crop-specific Recommendation → Balanced Fertilisation → Better Nutrient-use Efficiency

Farm mechanisation involves the use of machinery for land preparation, sowing, transplantation, spraying, harvesting and post-harvest operations. Mechanisation can improve timeliness and labour productivity, but small and fragmented holdings may make individual ownership of costly machines uneconomical. Custom Hiring Centres can address this problem by allowing several farmers to rent machinery when required.

✍️ Mains Line

Mechanisation policy should focus not merely on ownership of machinery, but on affordable access to appropriate machinery for small and marginal farmers.

🧬 26. Biotechnology, GM Crops and Digital Agriculture

Genetically modified crops contain genetic material altered through biotechnology to introduce or modify particular traits. One of the most important examples in Indian agriculture is Bt Cotton. The Bt trait is associated with genes derived from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis and provides resistance against specified insect pests. GM technology should therefore be understood separately from conventional breeding and hybridisation.

🎯 Prelims Trap

Bt Cotton → primarily associated with insect resistance.
Bt does not simply mean herbicide tolerance.

Digital agriculture uses satellite information, remote sensing, drones, GIS, mobile applications, sensors and artificial intelligence for agricultural decision-making. These technologies can assist in crop monitoring, weather advisories, pest identification, precision input application, insurance assessment and yield estimation. Agricultural management is therefore gradually moving from uniform input use towards data-driven and location-specific decisions.

Satellite + Drone + Sensor → Farm Data → AI / Analysis → Better Decisions → Precision Agriculture

Precision agriculture attempts to apply the right input, in the right quantity, at the right place and at the right time. It can improve the efficiency of water, fertilisers and plant protection chemicals while reducing unnecessary expenditure and environmental pressure.

🇮🇳 Andhra Pradesh Focus

Agricultural inputs in Andhra Pradesh should be linked with micro-irrigation for horticulture, quality seeds, soil testing, mechanisation services for small farmers, digital crop monitoring, weather advisories and technological support for horticulture and allied activities.

🌾 27. Agricultural Strategy and Sustainable Agriculture

Agricultural policy in India initially placed strong emphasis on increasing food production and achieving food security. Over time, however, the policy challenge became broader. Agriculture must now simultaneously address productivity, farmer profitability, natural resource conservation and climate resilience. The objective is therefore shifting from merely increasing production towards creating a productive, profitable and sustainable agricultural system.

Food Production → Food Security → Farmer Income → Resource Efficiency → Climate Resilience → Sustainable Agriculture

🌾 Green Revolution

The Green Revolution refers to the substantial increase in foodgrain production associated with the adoption of High-Yielding Varieties together with irrigation, fertilisers, pesticides, institutional credit, extension services and price and procurement support. Its strongest early impact in India was visible in wheat-producing regions with assured irrigation. The strategy played an important role in strengthening domestic food availability and reducing dependence on food imports.

🎯 Very Important

Green Revolution ≠ HYV seeds alone.

It was a package approach:
HYV + Irrigation + Fertilisers + Plant Protection + Credit + MSP/Procurement + Extension

⚠️ Limitations of the Green Revolution

The benefits of the Green Revolution were not distributed uniformly across crops and regions. Areas having assured irrigation, infrastructure and access to credit were better able to adopt the technology. Intensive cultivation also contributed in some regions to groundwater depletion, nutrient imbalance and excessive use of agricultural chemicals. Thus, although the Green Revolution addressed a major production challenge, it also highlighted the need for a more resource-efficient agricultural strategy.

♻️ 28. Sustainable Agriculture

Sustainable agriculture seeks to maintain agricultural production and farmer livelihoods without degrading the soil, water, biodiversity and other natural resources required by future generations. It combines economic viability with ecological conservation and social welfare. Sustainable agriculture therefore requires efficient water use, balanced nutrient management, crop diversification, soil conservation, suitable technology and climate-resilient farming practices.

Sustainable Agriculture = Productivity + Profitability + Environmental Conservation + Social Sustainability

🌿 Organic Farming and Natural Farming

Organic farming relies more heavily on organic manure, compost, crop rotation, biological nutrient management and biological pest control while reducing or avoiding synthetic chemical inputs according to the applicable production system. It should not be interpreted as farming without nutrients. Plants continue to require nutrients, but nutrient management is carried out through different sources and biological processes.

Natural farming generally aims to minimise dependence on externally purchased chemical inputs and promotes greater use of locally available biological and farm-derived resources. Organic farming and natural farming have several overlapping objectives, but they should not automatically be treated as identical concepts because their practices, certification frameworks and approaches may differ.

🐄 Integrated Farming System

An Integrated Farming System combines crop cultivation with one or more complementary enterprises such as livestock, dairy, poultry, fisheries or horticulture. Resources and wastes can be recycled between components—for example, crop residues may be used as livestock feed while livestock manure may return to the field. Integration can diversify farmer income, improve resource use and reduce dependence on a single agricultural activity.

Crops → Residues → Livestock → Manure → Crops

+ Dairy + Poultry + Horticulture + Fisheries

🌾 Crop Diversification

Crop diversification involves reducing excessive dependence on a small number of crops and expanding into a wider range of crops or allied agricultural activities. Diversification towards pulses, oilseeds, fruits, vegetables, horticulture, livestock and fisheries may improve income opportunities and reduce production risks. However, farmers will diversify sustainably only when alternative activities are supported by markets, storage, processing, transport and appropriate price signals.

✍️ Mains Line

Crop diversification is not merely an agronomic transition; it is also a market and institutional transition.

🌡️ 29. Climate-Resilient Agriculture

Agriculture is highly sensitive to changes in rainfall, temperature, water availability and extreme weather events. Climate-resilient agriculture seeks to improve the capacity of farming systems to anticipate, withstand, adapt to and recover from climatic stresses. Measures may include drought- and heat-tolerant varieties, micro-irrigation, crop diversification, soil and water conservation, weather advisories, insurance and appropriate changes in cropping patterns and sowing dates.

Concept Meaning Agriculture Example
Adaptation Adjusting to climate impacts Drought-tolerant varieties, changing sowing dates
Mitigation Reducing emissions or increasing carbon sinks Better residue management, carbon sequestration

💰 31. Minimum Support Price, CACP and Procurement

Minimum Support Price (MSP) is an administratively announced support price for specified agricultural crops. Its purpose is to provide farmers with a degree of protection against sharp falls in market prices and to give a price signal before sowing. MSP should not be confused with automatic procurement of all produce. Actual procurement depends on the commodity, procurement arrangements, quality standards and the reach of government agencies.

CACP → Recommends MSP
Government → Announces MSP
FCI / State Agencies → Procurement

Role of CACP

The Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) examines agricultural costs, price trends, demand and supply conditions and related factors before making recommendations on MSP. The Commission itself does not purchase crops and does not independently issue the final MSP notification. The final decision is taken by the Government after considering the recommendation and other policy inputs.

🎯 Prelims Trap

CACP recommends MSP.
Government announces MSP.
CACP does not procure foodgrains.

MSP and Cost of Production

Government policy has emphasised fixing MSP for mandated crops at a margin over the all-India weighted average cost of production. This should not be interpreted as a guarantee that every individual farmer receives a fixed multiple of his or her actual farm cost. Differences in farm size, irrigation, labour cost, productivity and location mean that individual production costs can vary significantly.

MSP vs Market Price

MSP is a policy support price, while market price is determined through actual buying and selling. Market prices may be above or below MSP. When open-market prices are attractive, farmers may prefer selling to private buyers. When prices fall significantly, procurement and other support mechanisms become more important for protecting farm incomes.

Procurement

Procurement is the actual purchase of eligible agricultural produce by government agencies at prescribed prices and quality standards. In the case of foodgrains such as wheat and paddy, agencies such as the Food Corporation of India and State procurement agencies play an important role. Procured foodgrains may enter the Central Pool and support food security and welfare distribution.

✍️ Mains Line

The effectiveness of MSP depends not merely on its announcement, but on procurement reach, market access, storage, infrastructure and the availability of competitive alternative markets.

🏪 32. APMC, Mandis and e-NAM

Agricultural Produce Market Committees (APMCs) are State-level market institutions created under State laws to regulate agricultural trade. Regulated mandis were intended to improve transparency, standardise weights and measures, supervise market transactions and protect farmers from unfair trading practices. Their actual performance, however, differs across States and commodities.

Why Regulated Markets?

Farmers historically faced problems such as arbitrary deductions, weak bargaining power, lack of reliable weighing facilities, delayed payment and dependence on local intermediaries. Regulated markets were created to bring agricultural trade within an organised framework. A well-functioning mandi should provide transparent bidding, reliable quality assessment, competition among buyers and timely payment.

Price Discovery

Price discovery is the process through which the market determines the price of a commodity. It works best when farmers have access to multiple buyers, transparent bidding, quality information and real-time market prices. Weak competition or information asymmetry can reduce farmers' bargaining power and result in poorer price realisation.

e-NAM

e-NAM, or National Agriculture Market, is an electronic trading platform designed to connect participating agricultural markets and improve transparency in price discovery. It does not mean that physical mandis disappear. Instead, the idea is to combine physical market infrastructure with digital bidding, wider buyer participation and electronic market information.

Farmer → Local Mandi → e-NAM → Wider Buyer Base → Better Price Discovery
🎯 Prelims Trap

e-NAM is not simply an online MSP procurement portal.
It is primarily a market-integration and electronic trading platform.

Assaying and Grading

Assaying means testing the quality of agricultural produce according to specified parameters. Quality assessment becomes particularly important in electronic trading because buyers may not physically inspect every lot before bidding. Better grading and assaying allow higher-quality produce to obtain better price signals and improve confidence in digital transactions.

Warehousing

Storage is a crucial part of agricultural marketing. A farmer without storage may be forced to sell immediately after harvest, when market arrivals are high and prices may be weak. Warehousing provides the option of delaying sale, subject to storage costs and future market conditions, and therefore improves marketing flexibility and bargaining power.

Harvest → Warehouse → Delayed Sale Option → Better Marketing Flexibility

Negotiable Warehouse Receipts

Warehouse receipts provide evidence that specified produce has been stored in an approved warehouse. Negotiable warehouse receipt systems can also help farmers obtain credit against stored produce. This can reduce the immediate pressure to sell after harvest and therefore links agricultural marketing with rural credit.

Farmer Producer Organisations

Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs) help small producers act collectively. Individual small farmers may have limited marketable surplus, high transport costs and weak bargaining power. Through an FPO, farmers may aggregate produce, purchase inputs collectively, undertake grading and storage, negotiate with buyers and participate more effectively in processing and marketing.

✍️ Mains Line

Agricultural marketing reform should strengthen competition, transparency, logistics, storage, grading, digital access and farmer aggregation rather than relying on a single institutional reform.
🇮🇳 Andhra Pradesh Focus

Paddy → procurement, milling and storage.
Chilli → grading, warehousing, processing and export linkages.
Horticulture → cold chain and rapid transport.
Aquaculture → cold chain, quality control and export markets.
Groundnut and other crops → transparent market access and processing.

💳 33. Agricultural Credit

Agriculture requires expenditure before income is realised. Farmers need money for seeds, fertilisers, labour, irrigation, machinery, transport and other operations, while income generally comes only after harvest and sale. Agricultural credit therefore bridges the gap between current expenditure and future farm income. Timely and affordable credit can help farmers adopt better inputs and technology, but poorly designed lending can also contribute to indebtedness.

Credit → Inputs → Production → Harvest → Sale → Repayment

Short-Term and Long-Term Credit

Short-term agricultural credit is mainly used for seasonal operations such as seeds, fertilisers, pesticides and labour. Medium- and long-term credit is generally used for investment in machinery, irrigation, land development, storage and other farm infrastructure. The distinction is important because crop finance and capital investment serve different purposes and involve different repayment periods.

Short Term → Run the crop
Long Term → Build the farm

Institutional and Non-Institutional Credit

Institutional sources include commercial banks, Regional Rural Banks, cooperative institutions and the wider NABARD-supported rural financial system. Informal sources include moneylenders, traders and other private lenders. Expansion of institutional credit is important because informal borrowing may involve high interest rates, tied transactions and weaker borrower protection.

NABARD

NABARD stands for National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development. It is an apex development financial institution with an important role in agriculture and rural development. Its functions include refinance, institutional development, rural infrastructure support and promotion of rural financial inclusion. NABARD should not be understood merely as a retail bank directly providing ordinary crop loans to every farmer.

🎯 Prelims Trap

NABARD → Apex / Refinance / Development role
RRB → Rural banking institution
PACS → Grassroots cooperative institution

Refinance

Refinance means financial support provided to eligible lending institutions against loans or investments that they have extended. It strengthens the capacity of banks and cooperative institutions to continue lending to agriculture and rural sectors. Refinance is therefore different from a farmer simply taking a second loan from the same bank.

Kisan Credit Card

The Kisan Credit Card system is designed to provide farmers with timely and flexible access to institutional credit for agriculture and allied activities. Instead of requiring a completely new application for every small seasonal requirement, an eligible farmer may receive an approved credit limit that can be used for crop-related expenditure according to applicable conditions.

KCC → Flexible and Timely Farm Credit

Cooperative Credit Structure

Cooperative institutions have historically played an important role in rural credit. A simplified short-term structure consists of State Cooperative Banks at the State level, District Central Cooperative Banks at the district level and Primary Agricultural Credit Societies at the local or village level. These institutions aim to bring formal credit closer to rural borrowers.

PACS

PACS stands for Primary Agricultural Credit Society. These are grassroots cooperative institutions operating close to rural borrowers. Their local presence can improve knowledge of members and facilitate crop-credit delivery. However, governance weaknesses, overdue loans, limited capital and inadequate professional capacity can reduce their effectiveness.

Regional Rural Banks

Regional Rural Banks were established to extend institutional banking services and credit to rural areas. Their traditional focus has included small and marginal farmers, agricultural labourers, rural artisans and other rural borrowers. RRBs should be distinguished from NABARD, which performs an apex development and refinance role.

Priority Sector Lending

Under the Priority Sector Lending framework, banks are required to direct part of their credit towards specified priority sectors. Agriculture is one of these priority sectors. The policy recognises that socially and economically important activities may otherwise receive inadequate credit if lending is based only on short-term commercial considerations.

SHG–Bank Linkage

Self-Help Groups are small community-based groups that promote savings, internal lending and financial discipline. Through bank linkage, SHGs can obtain access to formal finance without relying entirely on conventional collateral structures. This model has become important for financial inclusion, especially for small borrowers and rural households.

Agricultural Credit and FPOs

Credit is also important for Farmer Producer Organisations. FPOs may require working capital for procurement from members, aggregation, storage, processing, transport and marketing. Access to finance can therefore strengthen the ability of small farmers to participate in value chains collectively instead of selling individually.

Farmers → FPO → Credit → Aggregation → Storage / Processing → Market

Tenant Farmers and Access to Credit

Tenant farmers may face difficulties in accessing institutional farm credit when lending depends heavily on land ownership records or formal tenancy documentation. This can push cultivators towards informal lenders even when institutional credit is available in the wider rural economy. Improving access for genuine cultivators is therefore an important financial-inclusion issue.

🇮🇳 Andhra Pradesh Focus

Tenant cultivation, crop credit, SHG-bank linkage, cooperative credit, horticulture, aquaculture, irrigation investment and weather-related risks are important dimensions of agricultural finance in Andhra Pradesh.

Credit and Indebtedness

Credit can improve farm productivity when borrowed funds generate sufficient additional income. However, crop failure, low prices or repeated borrowing can create repayment stress. Agricultural credit policy should therefore be integrated with insurance, irrigation, extension services, storage, marketing and income-risk management.

✍️ Mains Line

Agricultural credit should increase productive capacity rather than merely increase indebtedness.

Loan Waiver vs Credit Reform

A loan waiver provides debt relief to eligible borrowers under a specific government decision. Agricultural credit reform, in contrast, seeks to improve the long-term availability, affordability and quality of rural finance. Loan waivers may provide immediate relief in distress situations, but they are not substitutes for sustainable institutional credit, risk management and income generation.

🍚 35. Food Security, Buffer Stocks & Public Distribution System

Food security exists when people have reliable physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food for an active and healthy life. It is therefore broader than food production alone. A country may produce adequate food overall, but households can still face food insecurity because of low income, high prices, weak distribution, nutritional deficiencies or disruptions caused by disasters and unemployment.

Four Dimensions of Food Security

Availability → Access → Utilisation → Stability
🎯 Prelims Trap

Food availability alone does not guarantee food security. Access, utilisation and stability are equally important.

Food Production vs Food Security

Food production refers to the quantity of food produced, whereas food security concerns whether people can actually obtain and properly use sufficient food over time. Income, market prices, transport, storage, public distribution and nutritional quality therefore matter along with agricultural output. This distinction is especially important in Mains questions dealing with poverty, hunger and malnutrition.

Role of FCI

The Food Corporation of India plays a central role in the foodgrain management system through procurement, storage, movement of stocks and support to public distribution. Foodgrains procured by FCI and State agencies form part of the Central Pool and can be used for welfare distribution and food-security requirements.

Farmer → Procurement → FCI / State Agencies → Central Pool → Storage & Movement → PDS / Welfare Schemes
🎯 Institutional Difference

CACP → recommends MSP
FCI → procurement, storage and foodgrain movement/distribution role

Buffer Stock

Buffer stock refers to foodgrain stocks maintained by public agencies to meet food-security needs, seasonal shortages and emergency requirements and to support market stability. Stocks built during periods of procurement can be released when required. However, excessive stocks also involve storage, handling and carrying costs, making efficient stock management important.

Public Distribution System

The Public Distribution System distributes foodgrains and other notified essential commodities through a network of Fair Price Shops. Under the Targeted Public Distribution System, support is directed to identified eligible households. The system therefore links public procurement and foodgrain stocks with household-level food access.

Procurement → Foodgrain Stocks → Allocation to States → Fair Price Shops → Eligible Households

National Food Security Act, 2013

The National Food Security Act, 2013 gives a legal basis to major food entitlements under the Targeted Public Distribution System. The Act provides coverage of up to 75 per cent of the rural population and up to 50 per cent of the urban population. It also provides different entitlement structures for Priority Households and Antyodaya Anna Yojana households.

Category Entitlement Remember
Priority Household (PHH) 5 kg foodgrains per person per month Per person
Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY) 35 kg foodgrains per household per month Per household

One Nation One Ration Card

One Nation One Ration Card improves portability of ration entitlements. It is particularly relevant for migrant workers and mobile households, because an eligible beneficiary can access entitled foodgrains away from the original place of registration, subject to the applicable system and authentication arrangements.

ONORC → Portability of Food Entitlements

Food Security vs Nutrition Security

Food security does not end with cereal availability. A household may receive adequate calories and still suffer from deficiencies of protein, iron, vitamins or other micronutrients. Nutrition security therefore requires dietary diversity, adequate micronutrients, safe food and effective maternal and child nutrition interventions in addition to basic food access.

Calories → Food Security → Diverse Diet → Micronutrients → Nutrition Security

Food Fortification

Fortification means adding specified micronutrients to commonly consumed foods. India's fortified-rice initiative is designed to address micronutrient deficiencies by supplying rice fortified with nutrients such as iron, folic acid and vitamin B12 through major welfare schemes. The current Central initiative for universal supply of fortified rice has been approved up to December 2028.

🎯 Prelims Trap

Food fortification = addition of micronutrients.
It is not the same as genetic modification.

Inclusion and Exclusion Errors

An inclusion error occurs when an ineligible person receives a benefit, while an exclusion error occurs when an eligible person fails to receive it. Digital systems, portability and improved beneficiary databases can reduce leakages, but reforms must also ensure that authentication and documentation requirements do not exclude genuine beneficiaries.

Error Meaning
Inclusion Error Ineligible person receives the benefit
Exclusion Error Eligible person is denied the benefit

Food Subsidy and Policy Trade-offs

Food subsidy supports affordable food access and the operation of the procurement-storage-distribution system. At the same time, procurement, storage and distribution involve substantial fiscal and logistical costs. A sound policy therefore needs to protect vulnerable households while improving efficiency, reducing leakage and avoiding unnecessary accumulation of stocks.

Procurement, Farmers and Consumers

Procurement has a dual role. It can support producer prices while also creating foodgrain stocks used for consumer food security. However, excessive concentration of procurement in a narrow group of crops may influence cropping choices and resource use. Food-security policy should therefore be aligned with crop diversification, water sustainability, pulses, millets and broader nutritional goals.

✍️ Mains Line

India must move from a narrow focus on grain security towards comprehensive nutrition security while preserving efficient procurement, storage and last-mile delivery.
🇮🇳 Andhra Pradesh Focus

Paddy procurement, milling and PDS are important links in Andhra Pradesh's food-security chain. Cyclones and floods can disrupt local supply and access, while migrant workers benefit from portability. Coastal, tribal and vulnerable populations also require strong last-mile delivery and nutrition-sensitive interventions.
📰 Current Affairs Link — 2026

SARTHAK-PDS was approved in May 2026 as an umbrella scheme combining support for intra-State foodgrain movement and Fair Price Shop margins with technology-led modernisation of the Public Distribution System. The Central outlay is ₹25,530 crore for the five-year period from 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2031.
